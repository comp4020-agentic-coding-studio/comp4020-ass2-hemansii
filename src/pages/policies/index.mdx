## Screenings are compulsory

Eleven of the twelve teaching weeks have a film attached, and attendance at the
Monday screening is a requirement of the course rather than a recommendation.

This is unusual and it is deliberate. The lecture assumes you have seen the
film. The workshop assumes the lecture. The Weekly Build Log asks what the film
changed in your language, and there is no version of that entry you can write
from a synopsis.

You do not have to attend the scheduled screening specifically. You have to have
watched the film, in full, before the Monday lecture. If you watch it elsewhere,
that is your business and nobody will ask.

### If you cannot watch a particular film

Tell Marisol before the week begins, not after. Two of the films carry content
that some students will reasonably not want to sit through, and there is an
alternative screening for every week of the course that covers the same
subsystem. Arranging one is routine and requires no explanation from you.

Nothing on this course requires you to watch something that will harm you. It
does require you to have watched *something*, and to have done the work.

## Extensions and late work

Ask before the deadline. An extension requested in advance is a scheduling
problem and is usually granted; the same request afterwards is a different
conversation with a worse outcome.

The Weekly Build Log is the exception and cannot be extended, because its
deadlines are the assessment. An entry written five weeks late is not a late
entry, it is a reconstruction, and the thing being marked — what you thought
that week, with that film fresh — no longer exists.

## Integrity

Submit a language you built.

The obvious version of the rule is that Quenya, Klingon, Dothraki, Na'vi and
every other language studied on this course belong to their creators, and
handing one in as your own is plagiarism of the ordinary kind. That case is
rare and easy.

The interesting case is borrowing, which this course actively encourages. Every
invented language borrows — from real languages, from other invented ones, from
whatever its author was reading. Herbert borrowed heavily and the result is not
diminished by it. What is required is that you write down what you took and
where from. The Portfolio and The Language both ask for this explicitly. An
undeclared borrowing is a problem; a declared one is scholarship.

### Generative tools

You may use them, and you must declare where.

A model will produce a plausible-looking phoneme inventory in four seconds. It
will also produce one that contradicts your own syllable structure, because it
is not tracking the eleven decisions you made in the preceding weeks — and the
crit is specifically designed to surface that. The stranger's read-back does not
care how the orthography was generated. It cares whether it works.

Where you have used a tool, say so, and say what you did with the output. Using
one to check your own consistency is sensible. Using one instead of deciding
anything is a strategy that fails in week twelve in front of an audience.

## Getting help

Workshop first — it is three hours a week with two tutors and the whole point of
the format is that problems get looked at with the language in front of
everybody. Almost nothing about your own language is better handled by email.

For anything that is not about your language — enrolment, adjustments,
extensions, or something going wrong outside the course — email Marisol
directly, and do it earlier than feels necessary.

If you have a registered access plan, it applies here in full, including to the
screening requirement and to the crit. Talk to us in week one so the
arrangements are made before they are needed rather than after.
