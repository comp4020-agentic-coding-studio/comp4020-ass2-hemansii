## The brief

> *Arrival* claims that learning a language can restructure how a mind
> experiences time. Say what would have to be true for that to be true.

Fifteen hundred to two thousand words.

The title of this assessment is a provocation and you are not obliged to accept
it. An essay arguing that the film is doing something more defensible than the
strong Whorfian reading suggests is entirely permitted, and past versions of
this argument have been among the better essays submitted. What is not permitted
is treating the film as though it were a paper with errors in it.

The first criterion carries a quarter of the marks and is where most essays are
lost in the first paragraph. The film's claim has to be reconstructed in its
strongest form before it is examined. If your statement of it is one a defender
of the film would reject as a caricature, everything after that is answering
nobody.

For the second criterion, two results, read properly. The colour-boundary work,
the spatial-frame research on languages that use absolute directions, the
grammatical gender studies — any of these, or others you find. Read the method
section. An essay that cites an effect without knowing what was measured is
doing the thing it accuses the film of.

## What you submit

One essay, fifteen hundred to two thousand words, with references in any
consistent style.

No glossing, no conlang material, and nothing about your own language. This is
the one assessment in the course that is not about the thing you are building,
and the fortnight it occupies is deliberate.
