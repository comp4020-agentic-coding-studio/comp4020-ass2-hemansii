Marc Okrand built Klingon for *The Search for Spock* in 1984, working outward
from a handful of lines an actor had already improvised in the first film. By
*The Undiscovered Country* there is enough of it to hold a scene, and the
courtroom sequence is the closest thing screen fiction has to a language being
used rather than displayed.

Okrand's design decision was to make Klingon systematically unlike English. Its
verbs take stacked affixes — a prefix carrying person and number for both
subject and object, then a queue of suffixes in a fixed order — so a single
Klingon word does work that English spreads across a clause. That is
agglutination: morphemes joined end to end, each one keeping its shape and doing
exactly one job.

The alternative is fusion, where one ending carries several categories at once
and cannot be cut apart — Latin's case endings, where a single vowel is doing
number and case and gender simultaneously. Or isolation, where you refuse to
glue anything and let word order and particles carry the load. You are picking
one of these on Thursday, and it decides how your language feels to read.
