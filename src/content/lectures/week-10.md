*The 13th Warrior* is not a good film, and it contains a sequence better than
anything else on this reading list. Ahmed, an Arab envoy travelling with a band
of Norsemen, sits at the fire night after night understanding nothing. The
camera returns to him across successive evenings. The Norse dialogue gradually
acquires English words at the edges, then in the middle, then entirely — and the
transition is played as his comprehension, not as a subtitling convention.

What it depicts is comprehensible input: acquisition driven by exposure to
language slightly beyond your current competence, in a context rich enough that
you can infer the rest. This is a real and well-supported mechanism. The film
compresses months into minutes and gives him fluency where he would realistically
get by, and it does one further thing right by having him acquire it by
listening rather than by studying.

The critical period is the harder claim. There is good evidence that first
language acquisition has a window and that missing it has severe and permanent
consequences; the evidence that adult second-language learners are categorically
barred from native competence is much weaker than the popular version suggests.
Adults learn languages. They learn them differently, usually more slowly in some
domains and faster in others.

This matters to you for a practical reason. In week twelve somebody who has
never seen your language will have to read a sentence of it aloud from your
orthography. If nothing about your language is learnable from exposure, you will
find that out in front of an audience.

## Outline

- the campfire sequence, and what it gets right about acquisition
- comprehensible input, and why immersion on its own is not magic
- the critical period — what the evidence supports and what it does not
